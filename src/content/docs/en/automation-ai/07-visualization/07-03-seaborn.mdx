## Why this matters

Some questions are about the **shape** of the data, not just totals: how spread out are the
amounts, and where are the busy and quiet spots? **seaborn** builds on matplotlib to
answer these in a line or two, directly from a DataFrame.

<CaseStudy>
	Daniela wants to know whether sandwiches are consistently expensive or just
	have a few big sales, and which category was busy in which week.
</CaseStudy>

## What seaborn adds

You still use matplotlib for the figure, titles, and saving. seaborn adds:

- Charts built from column names: `sns.boxplot(data=sales, x="categoria", y="monto")`.
- Good-looking default styles (`set_theme`).
- Statistical summaries, like a box plot, drawn for you.

<Mermaid
	diagram={`flowchart LR
  A["DataFrame in tidy form"] --> B["seaborn: pick columns"]

seaborn works best with **tidy data** (lesson 6.7): one row per observation, one column per variable.

## Two charts

<Example file="part07/03_seaborn.py" />

![Box plots of sale amounts for each category. Sandwich amounts are highest and widest. Tea and cake are lowest.](/charts/automation-ai/part07/03_boxplot.png)

A **box plot** shows the spread. The box covers the middle half of the values, the line inside is
the **median** (the middle value), and the whiskers extend to the usual range. Points beyond are unusual
values. Sandwich amounts sit highest, with the median at 3,200 colones, and tea sales are the lowest.

![Heatmap of sales by category and ISO week. Sandwiches in weeks 2 and 3 and juice in week 1 are the darkest cells. Cake in week 5 is the lightest.](/charts/automation-ai/part07/03_heatmap.png)

A **heatmap** colours a table by value. Darker means more. It comes from the pivot table of
lesson 6.5, with the numbers written in each cell, so nothing relies on colour alone. Week 5 is
short, because the file ends on 31 January, which explains its light cells.

## Reading carefully

- A box plot hides the number of points. Check the counts.
- In a heatmap, a light cell can mean "little" or "incomplete period". Know which.
- Don't use a colour scale in which both ends look alike. Use one hue from light to dark.

## Common mistakes

- **Using wide data.** Reshape to tidy rows first.
- **Rainbow palettes.** They have no order. Use one hue.
- **Over-reading small groups.** Few points make unstable boxes.
- **Forgetting incomplete periods,** like the short last week.
- **Letting `set_theme` change all later charts** without noticing.
