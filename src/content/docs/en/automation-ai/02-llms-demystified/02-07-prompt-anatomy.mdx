## Why this matters

Every lesson so far ended with a lousy prompt and a good one. Now you learn why the good
one worked. A **prompt** is the text you give an assistant. The assistant can only use
what is in that text. If a detail is not there, it will guess, and guesses are where
errors come from.

<CaseStudy>
	Daniela wants a summary of January sales. Her first prompt is "Summarise this
	CSV". The answer is generic. After she rewrites it with the six elements, the
	summary is useful and she can check it.
</CaseStudy>

## The six elements

| Element           | Question it answers                | Example                                            |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **Context**       | What situation am I in?            | "I am an analyst at a coffee company, on Windows." |
| **Task**          | What exactly do I want?            | "Summarise sales by category."                     |
| **Input**         | What does my data look like?       | "Here are the first 10 lines: ..."                 |
| **Constraints**   | What must it respect?              | "The separator is `;`. Do not delete duplicates."  |
| **Output format** | What shape should the answer have? | "A table, then three bullet points."               |
| **Verification**  | How will I know it is right?       | "Show the rows you used for each total."           |

You do not need all six every time. A quick question needs two. A task that affects
money or decisions should use all six.

## Why each element helps

- **Context** rules out wrong answers that fit another situation, such as Mac advice for a
  Windows user.
- **Task** prevents the assistant from guessing what you want. One clear task beats a
  list of five.
- **Input** gives it real material. Without it, the assistant invents plausible data.
- **Constraints** carry the facts that are obvious to you and invisible to it.
- **Output format** saves editing time and makes comparison easy.
- **Verification** makes errors visible, and gives you a checklist.

## Diagnosing a bad answer

When an answer disappoints, ask which element was missing:

| Symptom                             | Likely missing element |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Generic advice                      | Context                |
| It did something else               | Task                   |
| Invented details                    | Input                  |
| Ignored a rule you had in mind      | Constraints            |
| Hard to use, too long               | Output format          |
| You cannot tell whether it is right | Verification           |

## Hands-on

### Worked example

**Lousy:** "Summarise this CSV."

**Good:**

> I am an analyst at a coffee company. (context)
> Summarise total sales by category. (task)
> Here are the first 20 lines of the file: ... (input)
> The separator is a semicolon. Amounts are in colones. Do not remove duplicate rows,
> but list them. (constraints)
> Give a table with category, number of sales, and total, then three bullet points of
> observations. (output format)
> For each total, show the IDs of the rows you used, and say "unsure" if you cannot. (verification)

Notice that every line answers a question the assistant would otherwise have guessed.

### Your turn

Rewrite this prompt using all six elements. Use your own work in the answer.

> Write me a formula.

<Checkpoint question="Show a sample rewrite">
	I use Excel on Windows (context). Write a formula that returns the total of
	column D where column E equals "café" (task). Column D has amounts and column
	E has category names, rows 2 to 241 (input). Ignore blank cells, and do not
	use macros (constraints). Give the formula, then one sentence explaining each
	part (output format). Tell me how to test it against three rows I can check by
	hand (verification).
</Checkpoint>

## Common mistakes

- **Writing a wall of text.** Organise it. Short labelled lines work well.
- **Leaving out the input.** The assistant will invent it.
- **Asking for five things at once.** Split big jobs into steps.
- **Skipping verification because the task looks easy.** Easy tasks fail too.
- **Pasting secrets as "context".** Never paste passwords or keys (lessons 1.12 and 2.8).
- **Treating the prompt as magic words.** What works is clear information, not tricks.

## Ask your AI assistant

<PromptExample>
	<Fragment slot="lousy">Make my prompt better: summarise this CSV.</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="whatGoesWrong">
		The assistant produces a longer prompt full of generic phrases, such as "act
		as an expert analyst", which add words but no facts. It cannot add what only
		you know.
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="good">
		I will give you a draft prompt. Check it against six elements: context,
		task, input, constraints, output format, verification. For each element, say
		"present", "weak", or "missing", and quote the words that justify it. Then
		ask me up to three questions to fill the gaps. Do not rewrite the prompt
		yet. Draft: "Summarise this CSV."
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="whyItWorks">
		It turns the assistant into a reviewer using the **task** and the **output
		format**, and the **constraint** "do not rewrite yet" keeps you in charge of
		the facts. The questions extract the information only you have.
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="check">
		Check that each quote really appears in your draft. Answer the questions
		honestly, then ask for the rewritten prompt and compare it with the six
		elements again.
	</Fragment>
</PromptExample>
