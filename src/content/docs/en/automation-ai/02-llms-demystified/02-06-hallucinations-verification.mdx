## Why this matters

An assistant that sometimes invents facts, and sounds just as sure when it does, is
dangerous to trust blindly. It is also very useful when checked. The skill is not
avoiding the tool. The skill is to verify the right things cheaply.

<CaseStudy>
	Daniela asks an assistant to total the January sales. It returns a clean table
	with a grand total and says it checked for duplicates. The total is wrong by a
	few thousand colones, because the assistant never really added the numbers,
	and the duplicates were not removed.
</CaseStudy>

## What a hallucination is

A **hallucination** is a statement from an AI that sounds right but is false or not
supported: an invented quotation, a made-up web link, a function that does not exist,
a number that is not in the data.

It happens for the reason you saw in lesson 2.1. The model predicts plausible text. When
it lacks the facts, plausible text still comes out. It has no built-in alarm that says
"I do not know". Newer models do this less, but not never.

## When the risk is highest

- **Exact facts:** names, dates, numbers, citations, legal and tax details.
- **Arithmetic and counting** over many items. A model predicts digits, it does not
  calculate. Code or a spreadsheet does.
- **Obscure or recent topics,** outside its training (lesson 2.2).
- **Long inputs,** where parts are missed (lesson 2.3).
- **Requests that assume something false.** "Summarise the clause about X" when no such
  clause exists often yields an invented clause.

## A verification routine

<Mermaid
	diagram={`flowchart TD
  A["Get the answer"] --> B["Is it a fact, a number, or code?"]
  B --> C["Find the checkable claims"]
  C --> D["Check them against a source you trust"]
  D --> E{"All correct?"}
  E -- Yes --> F["Use it"]
  E -- No --> G["Correct it, or ask again with the right input"]`}
/>

1. **Mark the checkable claims.** Numbers, names, links, and quotes.
2. **Check a sample.** For a table of 240 rows, check five by hand.
3. **Check against the source.** Open the link, the file, or the documentation.
4. **Recalculate.** Have a spreadsheet or code do the arithmetic.
5. **Ask for the basis.** "Which rows did you use? Quote them."
6. **Match effort to risk.** A draft email needs a read. A tax figure needs a professional.

## Hands-on

### Worked example

Paste these three sales rows into an assistant and ask for their total, then compute it
yourself in a spreadsheet:

```text
T001;01/01/2024;C004;2200.00;jugo
T002;2024-01-01;C002;1500;pastel
T003;2024-01-01;C004;1400.00;té
```

The correct total is 5100. Did the assistant get it right? Did it show its work?

### Your turn

Which of these claims would you check first, and how?

1. "The best-selling category is coffee, with 38% of sales."
2. "The email's tone is friendly."
3. "See the guide on the example.com website, under coffee-tax-guide."

<Checkpoint question="Show the answers">
	Check 1 first: recompute the share from the data. Check 3 next: open the link,
	since it may not exist. The tone in 2 is a judgement you can read yourself.
</Checkpoint>

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting confidence.** The tone is the same when the answer is wrong.
- **Asking "are you sure?".** The assistant may just agree or reverse. Check the source.
- **Checking only the final answer.** Also check the inputs the assistant says it used.
- **Letting it do arithmetic.** Ask it to write a formula or a script, and run that.
- **Checking nothing because the first answers were right.** Errors are occasional, not
  predictable.

## Ask your AI assistant

<PromptExample>
	<Fragment slot="lousy">Summarise this CSV.</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="whatGoesWrong">
		The assistant produces a smooth summary with specific totals and trends,
		some of which are not in the file. You cannot tell which are real.
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="good">
		Below are 20 rows from a sales file (ID; date; customer; amount; category).
		Describe the columns, then give the total amount and the count of rows. For
		each number, show the rows you used. If you are not sure of a value, say
		"unsure" instead of estimating. Finish with a list of three things I should
		verify by hand.
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="whyItWorks">
		It provides the **input** and limits the **task**. Showing rows used makes
		mistakes visible, "unsure" removes pressure to guess, and the closing list
		is a built-in **verification** step.
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="check">
		Recompute the total in a spreadsheet and compare it. Check three of the
		cited rows against the file.
	</Fragment>
</PromptExample>

## Check yourself

<Checkpoint question="What is a hallucination?">
	A confident statement from an AI that is false or unsupported, such as an
	invented number or link.
</Checkpoint>

<Checkpoint question="Why should you not rely on an assistant for arithmetic over many rows?">
	It predicts digits instead of calculating. Use a formula or code and run it.
</Checkpoint>

<Checkpoint question="Name two quick ways to verify an answer.">
	Check a sample against the source, and recompute with a spreadsheet or code.
	Opening cited links also works.
</Checkpoint>
