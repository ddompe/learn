## Why this matters

You ask an assistant about a recent event, a new law, or a software version, and get a
confident answer that is out of date. It was not lying. It answered from what it learned
before a certain date. Knowing this helps you decide when to trust an answer and when to
ask for sources.

<CaseStudy>
	Daniela asks an assistant about this year's tax rules for coffee imports. The
	answer describes last year's rules, in the present tense.
</CaseStudy>

## Two phases

**Training** is the phase in which the model learns. It reads a huge amount of text and
adjusts its internal numbers, called **parameters**, until it predicts text well.
Training takes weeks on thousands of specialised computers and costs a great deal. It
happens once per model version.

**Inference** is the phase in which you use the model. You send a prompt, and the model
generates a reply using its fixed parameters. Nothing you type changes the model.

## The knowledge cutoff

The training data stops at some date, the **knowledge cutoff**. The model knows nothing
about events after that date. A model released today may have a cutoff many months
earlier, and people keep using it for a long time after release. So the gap between its
knowledge and today grows.

Two consequences:

- Questions about recent events get stale or invented answers.
- The model may not know about the newest versions of tools, which matters for code.

Your conversation does not train the model. When you correct it, it adjusts within the
conversation only (lesson 2.3).

## How products get current information

Many products wrap the model in extra steps:

1. They take your question and search the web or a document collection.
2. They paste the relevant results into the prompt.
3. The model writes the answer using that text.

This is often called **search** or **retrieval**. It reduces stale answers, but it adds
new risks: the search may fetch poor pages, and the model may misread them. Look for
links to sources and open them.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming the model knows today's date or news.** It may not, unless the product tells it.
- **Thinking your chat teaches the model.** It does not, unless the vendor says otherwise
  in its policy (lesson 2.8).
- **Trusting search-based answers without opening the sources.**
- **Asking about brand-new library versions without giving the documentation.** Paste the
  relevant part of the docs into the prompt.

## Ask your AI assistant

<PromptExample>
	<Fragment slot="lousy">
		What are the latest tax rules for coffee imports?
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="whatGoesWrong">
		The assistant answers from training data, stating old rules as if they were
		current, and often without any warning.
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="good">
		I need the current Costa Rican rules on coffee import taxes. If you cannot
		search the web in this conversation, say so first and describe only what you
		knew as of your training, with the approximate date. If you can search, give
		me links to the official sources you used. End with a list of what I should
		verify with an accountant.
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="whyItWorks">
		It sets the **constraint** that the assistant must state its limits first,
		requests **sources**, and ends with a **verification** list.
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="check">
		Open each link. Check that the page is an official source and that it
		supports the claim. For anything that affects money or law, confirm with a
		professional.
	</Fragment>
</PromptExample>

## Check yourself

<Checkpoint question="What is the difference between training and inference?">
	Training is when the model learns from data, once and at great cost. Inference
	is when you use the finished model.
</Checkpoint>

<Checkpoint question="Why does a model have a knowledge cutoff?">
	Its training data ends at a certain date, so it knows nothing about later
	events.
</Checkpoint>

<Checkpoint question="How do products give models current information?">
	They search for relevant pages or documents and paste the results into the
	prompt.
</Checkpoint>
