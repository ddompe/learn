## Why this matters

An AI assistant seems to understand you. If you believe it does, you will trust it too
much. If you think it is magic, you cannot predict when it will fail. A correct mental
model lets you use it well and notice trouble early.

<CaseStudy>
	Daniela asks an assistant to describe Café Central's January sales. The answer
	reads smoothly and confidently. To judge it, she needs to know what the
	assistant did to produce those sentences.
</CaseStudy>

## Tokens

A **large language model** (LLM) is a program trained on a vast amount of text to
predict what text comes next. ChatGPT, Copilot, and Claude are products built on LLMs.

An LLM does not read letters or whole words. It reads **tokens**: small pieces of text.
A token can be a short word, part of a longer word, or a punctuation mark. In English,
one token is about four characters, or three quarters of a word. Spanish text usually
needs more tokens for the same meaning.

For example, "Café Central sells coffee" may split into pieces such as "Caf", "é",
" Central", " sells", " coffee". The exact split depends on the model.

## Next-token prediction

An LLM does one thing: given the tokens so far, it chooses a likely next token. Then it
adds that token to the text and repeats.

<Mermaid
	diagram={`flowchart LR
  A["Text so far"] --> B["Predict the next token"]
  B --> C["Add it to the text"]
  C --> A`}
/>

To see the idea, here is a toy model. It "reads" a tiny text and counts which word
follows which. Then it predicts the most common follower.

<Example file="part02/01_next_word.py" />

After "the", the word "cafe" followed three times and "shop" once, so the toy model
predicts "cafe". It has no idea what a cafe is. It only counts.

A real LLM does something similar in spirit, but at an enormous scale. It has learned
patterns from billions of pages, and it looks at much more than the last word. That is
why it can write fluent paragraphs, translate, and follow instructions.

## What follows from this

- **It produces plausible text, not checked facts.** Plausible and true are different.
  Lesson 2.6 returns to this.
- **It does not "look up" things by itself.** What it knows comes from its training, unless
  the product adds a search step (lesson 2.2).
- **The same prompt can give different answers.** The choice of next token has some
  randomness.
- **Wording matters.** A different prompt changes the starting text, so it changes the
  prediction. This is why prompting is a skill (lesson 2.7).

Do not conclude that LLMs are useless. Predicting text well turns out to be very useful
for drafting, summarising, explaining, and writing code. The limits just need to be known.

## Hands-on

### Your turn

Change the toy text in your head. Suppose the text were: "the cafe sells coffee and the
cafe sells coffee and the cafe sells tea".

1. What would the toy model predict after "sells"?
2. Is that prediction a fact about the world?

<Checkpoint question="Show the answers">
	1. "coffee", because it followed "sells" twice and "tea" once. 2. No. It
	reflects only the text the model saw. A real model is the same on a larger
	scale: it reflects its training text.
</Checkpoint>

## Common mistakes

- **Believing the model understands like a person.** It predicts text. Understanding is a
  useful way to talk, but a poor way to predict failures.
- **Assuming the answer was looked up.** Unless the product searches, it was generated.
- **Expecting the same answer twice.** Variation is normal.
- **Judging by fluency.** Smooth writing says nothing about accuracy.

	reads and writes.
