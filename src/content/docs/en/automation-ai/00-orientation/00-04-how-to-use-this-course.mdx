## Why this matters

If you know how lessons are laid out, you can study faster. You will also know which
parts to skim and which parts to slow down for.

</CaseStudy>

## How a lesson is built

Every lesson has the same sections, in the same order.

<Mermaid
	diagram={`flowchart LR
  A["Goals"] --> B["Why this matters"]
  B --> C["Concepts"]
  C --> D["Hands-on"]
  D --> E["Common mistakes"]
  E --> F["Ask your AI assistant"]
  F --> G["Check yourself"]
  G --> H["Going deeper"]`}

| Section               | What it is for                               |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Goals                 | What you will be able to do afterwards.      |
| Why this matters      | The business problem, tied to Café Central.  |
| Concepts              | The mental model. Read this slowly.          |
| Hands-on              | A worked example, then an exercise for you.  |
| Common mistakes       | Failures others hit, so you can skip them.   |
| Ask your AI assistant | A lousy prompt and a good one, side by side. |
| Check yourself        | A few questions with answers you can reveal. |
| Going deeper          | Optional reading.                            |

Try to answer before you reveal an answer. Guessing and being wrong teaches more than
reading the answer.

## Why every lesson ends with an AI prompt

AI assistants are useful, but they write confidently even when they are wrong. The
prompt section shows two things.

1. A **prompt** is the text you type to an AI assistant. A vague prompt gets a vague
   or invented answer. A specific prompt gets a better one.
2. Whatever the answer, you must **check** it.

So each prompt section has five parts: a lousy prompt, what goes wrong, a good prompt,
why it works, and how to check the answer. You see about 80 examples over the course,
and by the end, writing good prompts becomes a habit.

If you only keep one habit from this course, keep the last one: **check the answer**.
The assistants change often, but this habit stays useful.

## Hands-on

### Practise on a prompt section

Look at the prompt section of lesson 0.3. Answer these in your own words:

1. What was missing from the lousy prompt?
2. What did the good prompt add?
3. What would you check in the answer?

<Checkpoint question="Show a sample answer">
	The lousy prompt gave no file excerpt and no instructions about assumptions.
	The good prompt added the excerpt, the separator, and a request to list
	assumptions. You would check three rows by hand against what the assistant
	claimed.
</Checkpoint>

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping "Check the answer".** It is the most valuable part. An unchecked answer is a
  guess.
- **Reading without doing.** The exercises are where the learning happens.
- **Copying prompts word for word.** Use them as models, then change the details to
  match your own work.
- **Racing ahead.** Short lessons are meant to be done one at a time.

## Ask your AI assistant

<PromptExample>
	<Fragment slot="lousy">How do I use this course?</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="whatGoesWrong">
		The assistant has never seen this course, so it invents a plausible
		structure with modules and quizzes that may not exist.
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="good">
		I am starting a free online course for business professionals. Each lesson
		has these sections: Goals, Why this matters, Concepts, Hands-on, Common
		mistakes, Ask your AI assistant, Check yourself, Going deeper. I use Excel
		daily and have never coded. Suggest a 20-minute study routine for one
		lesson, in 5 bullet points. Say which sections I could skim if I am short on
		time.
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="whyItWorks">
		The good prompt supplies **context** (who you are and what the course
		contains), since the assistant cannot see the course. It states a
		**constraint** (20 minutes) and an **output format** (5 bullets).
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="check">
		Compare the suggested routine with the real lesson page. Does it skip
		Hands-on or Check yourself? If so, it is not a good routine, because those
		are the sections that teach.
	</Fragment>
</PromptExample>
