## Why this matters

You already work with data every day. You build spreadsheets, refresh reports, and copy
numbers from one place to another. Some of that work is repetitive, and you have
probably wondered whether a computer could do it for you.

Today it often can. An **AI assistant** is a chat tool that can write and explain
programs for you. It is tempting to stop there: ask, paste, run. That gives you a
demo script, something that worked once, on your computer, today. A real solution has
to keep working next month, on a colleague's computer, with a file that looks slightly
different. That takes basic engineering: results you can reproduce, code you can test,
changes you can track, and a setup other people can recreate.

All of that comes from software engineering principles. An assistant will not apply
them unless you know to ask, and you cannot tell whether it did unless you understand
them yourself.

This course teaches those principles first, in plain language. Then it uses them to
automate real work.

## How every lesson works

Every lesson has the same sections in the same order: goals, why it matters, concepts,
hands-on practice, common mistakes, a prompt section, a short self-check, and optional
reading. Lesson 0.4 explains each section. This first lesson has no self-check, because there is nothing to test yet.

The prompt section, "Ask your AI assistant", appears on every page. It shows a lousy
prompt and a good prompt for the subject of that page, why the good one works, and how
to check the answer. For example, on a lesson about file paths you would see prompts
about file paths, not generic ones. By the end you will have seen about 80 examples.

## Why the tools are fixed

Most tutorials show three ways to do everything and leave you to choose. That is
confusing when you are new. This course is **opinionated**: it picks one set of tools
and teaches only that set.

- **One AI assistant.** From Part 3 on, you use GitHub Copilot inside VS Code. It has a
  free tier, so you need no paid account to follow the core course.
- **One way to run Python.** You install it with a tool called uv, which works without
  administrator rights on a company laptop.
- **One case study.** Every example uses Café Central, so you spend your effort on the
  new idea, not on understanding a new dataset.

Once you know the fundamentals, you can switch tools easily. A fixed stack just keeps
you from getting lost on the way.

## Choose your learning path

A **learning path** is a list of lessons for a specific goal. You do not have to take
the whole course. The three paths are nested: each one contains the one before it.

## What you will be able to do at the end

On the core path, you will be able to:

- Explain how files, folders, text encodings, and the terminal work.
- Explain what an AI model does, where it fails, and how to check its answers.
- Use an AI assistant to write Python, then read, test, and fix what it wrote.
- Clean and combine messy business files, such as CSV and Excel.
- Produce charts and reports that update themselves.
- Run a complete monthly report with a single command.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping the fundamentals to get to Python faster.** It feels slower, but most
  stuck moments later come from a gap in Parts 1 and 2, such as a wrong file path or
  garbled accents. Take the shorter path instead of skipping content.
- **Picking the advanced path first.** Part 10 builds on everything before it. Without
  the core path, it will not make sense.
- **Treating the AI assistant as an authority.** It writes confident text whether it is
  right or wrong. Every lesson ends with a way to check its answer.

## Ask your AI assistant

<PromptExample>
	<Fragment slot="lousy">Should I learn Python?</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="whatGoesWrong">
		The assistant lists generic pros and cons of Python, mentions data science
		and web development, and ends with "it depends on your goals". Nothing in
		the answer connects to your job, so you learn nothing you could not find in
		a general article.
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="good">
		I work as a finance analyst. I use Excel and Power Query every day, and I
		have never written code. Every month I spend about three hours combining
		five CSV files and fixing the date columns by hand. Should I learn Python
		for this, or is there a better way? Give me a recommendation in one short
		paragraph, then list the two things that would change your recommendation.
		Tell me which parts of your answer you are unsure about.
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="whyItWorks">
		The good prompt gives **context** (your role and tools), a specific **task**
		(combining files and fixing dates), the amount of time at stake, and an
		**output format** (a short recommendation plus two conditions). It also asks
		the assistant to say what it is unsure about. Lesson 2.7 covers these
		elements in full.
	</Fragment>
	<Fragment slot="check">
		Check that the recommendation mentions your actual task, not just "data".
		Then ask yourself whether the "two things that would change the
		recommendation" are true for you. If the assistant suggests Power Query or a
		formula, test that first.
	</Fragment>
</PromptExample>
