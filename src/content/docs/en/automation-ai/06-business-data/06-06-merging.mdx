## Why this matters

Data about one business is spread across tables: sales in one, customers in another.
In Excel you bring them together with `VLOOKUP` or `XLOOKUP`. In pandas, you use `merge`,
which is more powerful and can show what did **not** match, the part that Excel hides
behind `#N/A`.

<CaseStudy>
	The sales table has a customer ID, but not the name or city. The customer list
	has those. Daniela wants sales by city, and a list of sales whose customer
	cannot be found.
</CaseStudy>

## Keys and join types

A **key** is a column that identifies a row, such as the customer ID. A **join** combines
rows from two tables where the keys match. The join type decides what happens to rows
without a match.

| Join    | Keeps                                               | Excel analogy                    |
| ------- | --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| `inner` | Only rows with a match in both tables               | Filter out every `#N/A`          |
| `left`  | All rows of the left table, with blanks if no match | `XLOOKUP` with a not-found value |
| `right` | All rows of the right table                         | The reverse of `left`            |
| `outer` | All rows of both                                    | No direct equivalent             |

## The two classic failures

1. **Rows multiply.** If the key is **not unique** in the right table, each sale matches
   several rows, and the total grows. Check `is_unique` first, and compare row counts.
2. **Rows vanish.** An `inner` join silently drops sales without a customer. Use `left` and
   inspect the non-matches.

## Common mistakes

- **Not checking the row count** after a join.
- **Using inner when you wanted to see the gaps.**
- **Joining on text that differs by case, spaces, or accents.** Clean the keys first.
- **Duplicate keys in the lookup table.**
- **Columns with the same name in both tables.** pandas adds suffixes; rename on purpose.
